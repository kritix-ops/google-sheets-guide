# Conditional formatting: rule-based and formula-based

A campaign log without conditional formatting is a wall of numbers. Sixty rows of Spend, Revenue, ROI. The losing campaign and the breakout winner sit one above the other and look identical. Conditional formatting is the visual layer that turns that wall into something a human reads in two seconds: red on rows that lost money, green on rows that beat target, gray on paused campaigns. It costs nothing at runtime when used correctly, and it's the cheapest way to make a dashboard usable.

Pair this with validation (lesson 2.4) and you have the full input-output story: validation refuses to let bad values in; conditional formatting makes the values that did land legible at a glance. Neither replaces the other.

## Rule-based vs formula-based: the two big shapes

`Format → Conditional formatting` opens a panel with one toggle that matters: **Single color** or **Color scale**, and inside Single color, the dropdown that decides whether the rule is *condition-based* or *formula-based*.

| Shape | When to use | API conditionType |
|---|---|---|
| Greater / less than a number | Single-column thresholds: Spend > 500, ROI < 0 | `NUMBER_GREATER`, `NUMBER_LESS` |
| Between two numbers | Banding: ROI between 0 and 0.5 looks meh | `NUMBER_BETWEEN` |
| Text contains | Status columns: paused, killed, blocked | `TEXT_CONTAINS` |
| Date is | This week, before today | `DATE_BEFORE`, `DATE_AFTER` |
| Custom formula is | Anything that compares more than one cell or applies cross-row logic | `CUSTOM_FORMULA` |

The decision tree: if the rule is "this cell on its own crosses some threshold", use a condition. If the rule needs to compare the cell to *another cell on the same row* (revenue vs spend, today vs date, status vs target), reach for custom formula.

## Custom formulas: per-row evaluation, with a relativity catch

The custom formula option is where conditional formatting earns its keep. The formula is written as if it applies to the *first* cell in the range. When the rule is applied to `G2:G61`, you write `=G2>F2`. Sheets re-evaluates that formula at every cell in the range, treating the references *relatively*. So the rule fires on G2 when `G2>F2`, on G3 when `G3>F3`, on G4 when `G4>F4`, all the way down. You wrote one formula and you got 60 row-by-row comparisons.

The relativity is the same model as a dragged formula. Which means the same `$` rules apply. Lock the column when you want every row to compare against a fixed value: `=G2>$J$1` highlights any revenue greater than the threshold in `J1`. Lock the row when you want every cell in a row to share the same lookup: `=$D2="Google"` highlights the entire row whenever column D says Google.

The trap: writing `=$G$2>$F$2` by accident. With both anchors locked, the rule evaluates the same comparison once and applies the result to every cell in the range. You either get the whole range green or none of it, and you'll spend ten minutes wondering what's wrong.

## Color scales: gradients that say "compared to what"

Flip the panel's top toggle from **Single color** to **Color scale** and the rule type changes shape. Instead of "fire the format when the condition is true", a color scale paints *every* cell in the range with a color interpolated from its value's position between a Min, an optional Mid, and a Max. The output is a gradient. The reader's eye reads relative magnitude before reading any number.

Three things pros use color scales for: ROI heatmaps (where breakeven is the visual axis), week-over-week delta tables (positive vs negative deltas pop on opposite sides of zero), and percentile rankings across a metric column (which campaigns are top decile this week). The team's "Pacing" tab and "Weekly ROI" tab are both color-scale dashboards.

### The five ways to anchor Min, Mid, and Max

The dropdown next to each of Min / Mid / Max controls *what number that endpoint snaps to*. Picking the wrong anchor is how a heatmap turns into a washed-out smear.

| Type | Behavior | When to use |
|---|---|---|
| Min/Max value (default) | Min snaps to the lowest cell value in the range, Max to the highest | Relative comparison across the range. "Show me the spread, whatever the spread is." |
| Number | You pin Min and Max to literal numbers (e.g., Min=0, Max=10) | Absolute thresholds you actually care about. ROI > 1 is profitable, full stop. |
| Percent | Min and Max are positions along the range as a percent (e.g., 10% and 90%) | Smoothing out outliers. Anything below the 10% mark is solid red, above the 90% mark is solid green. |
| Percentile | Min and Max are percentile ranks of the values (e.g., 10th and 90th percentile) | Robust against a few outlier rows. The hot vertical doesn't dominate the palette. |
| Formula | You compute Min or Max with a formula (e.g., `=AVERAGE(B:B)-STDEV(B:B)`) | Statistical thresholds. Color any cell more than one standard deviation from the mean. |

The Mid setting is optional and changes everything. Leave it blank and you get a two-stop gradient (red to green, washed through whatever the linear interpolation is). Set it to a Number and you get a three-stop *diverging* palette: red below Mid, white at Mid, green above Mid. For ROI, the obvious Mid is `1.0` (breakeven). Now the visual axis matches the business axis: red is losing money, green is making money, white is the line.

### Worked example: ROI heatmap on the campaign log

Picture the campaign log with an extra ROI column computed as `=G2/F2` (revenue over spend). The values land roughly between `0` (total loss) and `3` (a great day). With Min/Max = Min/Max value, a single `5.0` row dominates the gradient and everything else looks washed out. Pin the endpoints to numbers and the heatmap becomes legible:

| Setting | Value | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Min | Number `0` | Total loss anchors solid red |
| Mid | Number `1.0` | Breakeven is the white visual axis |
| Max | Number `3.0` | A 3x ROI day anchors solid green |

A reviewer scrolling the ROI column now reads the column in two seconds: blocks of green are the buyer's good days, blocks of red are the bad days, white rows are roughly breakeven. No number-squinting required.

## Performance: cheap by default, expensive when paired with volatile functions

Conditional formatting is fast on its own. Sheets evaluates the rule once per cell when the cell or its dependencies change. The cost goes up when the formula references **volatile functions** like `TODAY`, `NOW`, `OFFSET`, `INDIRECT`, or `RAND`, because those re-fire on every recalculation. A rule like `=A2<TODAY()-30` running on a 60-row range is fine. The same rule running on `A:A` over a 50,000-row aggregator workbook with five other volatile rules will make the whole spreadsheet feel laggy.

Two practical guards: bound the range (`A2:A1000`, not `A:A`, same discipline as lesson 2.1) and prefer non-volatile alternatives where possible (a static cutoff date in `J1` is cheaper than `TODAY()`). Track 4 lesson 2 covers the full cost model when a workbook accumulates volatile formatting across many sheets.

## Used in the wild

In the team's actual workbooks:

- **Loss rows in red.** A custom-formula rule of `=$G2<$F2` on the entire data range paints any losing row red across all columns. The dashboard reviewer scans for red and asks "what happened on Tuesday?" instead of squinting at numbers.
- **ROI > target green, ROI < target amber.** Two rules on the ROI column, ordered by priority. Targets live in a `Config` tab and the rule references them by absolute ref, so changing the target in one place re-paints every dashboard.
- **Paused campaigns grayed out.** A `TEXT_CONTAINS` rule on the Status column, with the entire row range as the target via custom formula `=$H2="paused"`. Paused rows visually fade so they don't compete with active campaigns for attention.

## Pro pitfalls

- **Ranges that don't extend when new rows arrive.** A rule applied to `F2:F61` covers exactly 60 rows. Tomorrow's row 62 lands without the formatting. Either extend the range (`F2:F1000`) or build a habit of re-checking conditional-format ranges when a tab grows. Tables (Track 1 lesson 17) auto-extend formatting and sidestep this entirely.
- **Rules duplicated by paste.** Copy-paste a row from one tab to another and the conditional-format rules come along, sometimes with shifted ranges. Two months later the workbook has 14 versions of "the same" rule and you can't tell which one is live. Audit periodically: open the panel and read the list.
- **The "color = state" anti-pattern.** Painting a cell yellow to mean "needs review" works for one person on one day. Three months later, no one remembers what yellow means. Real status belongs in a Status column with a documented vocabulary. The conditional-format rule reads the column. Color is the visual, not the storage.
- **Custom formulas with the wrong anchoring.** `=$G$2>$F$2` looks right and is wrong: with both anchors absolute, the rule evaluates once and applies that single boolean to every cell. The rule pattern for "this row is profitable" is `=G2>F2` (no anchors) or `=$G2>$F2` (column-locked, useful when extending the rule across columns).
- **Volatile functions multiplying cost.** A rule with `TODAY()` on a 50,000-row range, paired with four other rules that also call `TODAY()`, recalcs all 250,000 evaluations on every edit. Either bound the range tightly or stash the volatile value in one cell and reference *that* cell from every rule.

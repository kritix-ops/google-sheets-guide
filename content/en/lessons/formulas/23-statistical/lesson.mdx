# Statistical functions: see past the average

`AVERAGE` is the laziest statistic: a single outlier moves it, a skewed distribution hides behind it. This lesson covers the functions that paint a more honest picture: median and mode for "typical," percentile and quartile for distribution shape, `STDEV` and `VAR` for spread, `RANK` and `LARGE` for "where in the order," and `CORREL`/`TREND` for relationships between variables.

In adtech the data is almost always skewed. A few mega-campaigns drive most of the spend; a handful of bad days drag the mean. Statistical functions are how you separate "what's normal" from "what's noisy." A 6-number distribution summary (min, Q1, median, Q3, max, 90th percentile) on every dashboard tab tells leadership ten times more than five different "average" numbers, and takes about 60 seconds to wire up.

## Center: MEDIAN, MODE

`MEDIAN(range)` is the middle value when sorted. Unlike `AVERAGE`, it ignores outliers and gives you a robust "typical."

<FunctionRef name="MEDIAN" />

```
=MEDIAN(E2:E13)        → typical campaign spend
=AVERAGE(E2:E13)       → mean campaign spend (skewed by big spends)
```

If the median is much smaller than the mean, you have a long-tail distribution: a few large campaigns are pulling the average up. Useful diagnostic before quoting "average campaign spend" in a report.

`MODE(range)` returns the most frequent value (or `#N/A` if all values are unique). Rarely useful for continuous data like spend; more useful for categorical-coded numerics ("most common bid amount" when everyone uses round numbers).

## Spread: STDEV, VAR, MIN, MAX

`STDEV(range)` is the sample standard deviation. `VAR(range)` is the variance (`STDEV` squared). High `STDEV` means values are spread out; low means they cluster around the mean.

<FunctionRef name="STDEV" />

```
=STDEV(E2:E13) / AVERAGE(E2:E13)    → coefficient of variation
```

The coefficient of variation (`STDEV`/`MEAN`) is unitless. Useful for comparing spread across metrics with different scales: CV of 0.3 means "values typically vary ±30% from the mean." The team tracks CV per buyer as a stability metric: stable buyers are predictable, unstable ones need bigger budgets to absorb variance.

`MIN(range)` and `MAX(range)` give the extremes. `MAX - MIN` is the range. Combined with median and quartiles, you get the 5-number summary.

## Distribution shape: PERCENTILE, QUARTILE

`PERCENTILE(range, p)` returns the value at percentile `p` (between 0 and 1). `PERCENTILE(E2:E13, 0.9)` is the 90th-percentile spend: 90% of campaigns spend less than this.

<FunctionRef name="PERCENTILE" />

```
=PERCENTILE(E2:E13, 0.9)    → 90th percentile spend
=PERCENTILE(E2:E13, 0.1)    → 10th percentile spend
```

`QUARTILE(range, q)` is the same idea with `q` as 0/1/2/3/4: minimum, Q1, median, Q3, maximum. `QUARTILE(E2:E13, 3)` is the 75th percentile.

The classic distribution check, the one that pays for itself on every dashboard:

| Statistic | Formula | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Min | `MIN(E2:E13)` | Worst case |
| Q1 (25th) | `QUARTILE(E2:E13, 1)` | Bottom quarter cutoff |
| Median (50th) | `MEDIAN(E2:E13)` | Typical |
| Q3 (75th) | `QUARTILE(E2:E13, 3)` | Top quarter cutoff |
| Max | `MAX(E2:E13)` | Best case |
| 90th | `PERCENTILE(E2:E13, 0.9)` | Outlier threshold |

## Position: RANK, LARGE, SMALL

`RANK(value, range)` returns the position of `value` in `range` (1 = highest). Useful for "where does this campaign rank?"

<FunctionRef name="RANK" />

<FunctionRef name="LARGE" />

```
=RANK(F2, F$2:F$13)     → row 2's revenue rank, descending
=RANK(F2, F$2:F$13, 1)  → ascending instead (third arg is order)
```

`LARGE(range, n)` returns the `n`th-largest value. `LARGE(F2:F13, 1)` is the max; `LARGE(F2:F13, 2)` is the second-highest. `SMALL` is the same but ascending.

A common adtech pattern: top-3 by revenue without sorting the whole list.

```
=LARGE(F2:F13, 1)    → highest revenue
=LARGE(F2:F13, 2)    → second-highest
=LARGE(F2:F13, 3)    → third-highest
```

Pair `LARGE` with `INDEX`/`MATCH` to find the *campaign* behind the value, not just the number: `=INDEX(C2:C13, MATCH(LARGE(F2:F13, 1), F2:F13, 0))` returns the vertical of the top-revenue campaign.

## Relationships: CORREL, TREND, SLOPE

`CORREL(range1, range2)` is the Pearson correlation between two parallel ranges. Returns a value between -1 and 1.

<FunctionRef name="CORREL" />

```
=CORREL(E2:E13, F2:F13)
```

How strongly does spend predict revenue? `0.6+` is strong; `0.3` is weak; near 0 is no relationship; negative is "more spend, less revenue." The team uses this to validate a hypothesis: "Does our scaling rule actually work? When we spent more, did we earn more?"

`TREND` and `SLOPE` fit a linear regression. `SLOPE(known_y, known_x)` returns the slope, which answers "how much extra revenue per extra dollar of spend." `FORECAST(x, known_y, known_x)` extrapolates: given the regression of `known_y` on `known_x`, what y-value corresponds to `x`?

## Used in the wild

- **Median-vs-mean comparison** to detect outlier-heavy distributions. The team tracks both for spend, revenue, and ROI on every dashboard.
- **5-number distribution summaries** (MIN/Q1/MEDIAN/Q3/MAX) for every metric, as the second tab of every dashboard. Tells leadership the shape, not just the average.
- **Percentile thresholds** for automatic "winners" / "losers" filters. Top-decile and bottom-decile campaigns get flagged for review.
- **`CORREL` between spend and revenue per buyer.** Buyers with high correlation are reliable scalers; buyers with low correlation either pick winners (great when wins land, but unpredictable) or are randomly profitable (more questions than answers).
- **`STDEV` / `MEAN` coefficient of variation** as a "stability" metric for buyers and verticals. Stable buyers are predictable; unstable ones need bigger budgets to absorb variance.
- **`LARGE` / `SMALL`** for top-N and bottom-N reports. Often combined with `INDEX`/`MATCH` to find which campaign hit the top spot, not just the value.

## Pro pitfalls

- **`STDEV` vs `STDEVP`.** `STDEV` is the *sample* standard deviation (divides by n-1); `STDEVP` is the *population* (divides by n). Most adtech data is a sample, so use `STDEV`. Same distinction for `VAR` vs `VARP`.
- **`PERCENTILE` has variants.** `PERCENTILE.INC` (default `PERCENTILE`) includes endpoints; `PERCENTILE.EXC` excludes. They give slightly different values; for most reporting it does not matter, but if you are benchmarking against a tool that uses one or the other, match it.
- **`MEDIAN` of an even-sized range averages the middle two.** With 12 campaigns, `MEDIAN` is the average of the 6th and 7th sorted values, not a "real" data point.
- **`CORREL` on tiny samples is misleading.** With 5 data points, a correlation of 0.7 might be noise. You need at least 20-30 paired observations before correlation is meaningful.
- **`CORREL` does not tell you direction of causation.** Spend and revenue might correlate because spend causes revenue, OR because we spend more on already-promising campaigns, OR both. Use as a hypothesis check, not a causal claim.
- **`RANK` ties give the same rank, then skip.** Two campaigns tied for 1st both rank 1; the next is rank 3, not rank 2. `RANK.AVG` averages tied ranks (e.g., two tied for 1st both get 1.5). Pick the variant that matches your team's reporting convention.
- **`PERCENTILE` of `0` and `1`** are `MIN` and `MAX`. Useful when you want a one-formula `MIN`/`MAX` call, but not how most people use the function.
- **`MODE` returns `#N/A` if all values are unique.** Common on continuous data like spend. Wrap with `IFNA` if the dashboard cell must not error.

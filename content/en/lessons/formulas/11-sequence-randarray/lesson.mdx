# Generators: SEQUENCE, RANDARRAY, MAKEARRAY

Most formulas you have met so far transform a range that already exists. *Generators* do the opposite: they invent the range. `SEQUENCE` produces a counted list of numbers. `RANDARRAY` produces random decimals. `MAKEARRAY` calls a `LAMBDA` once for every cell of a grid. All three are array-aware on their own (no `ARRAYFORMULA` wrapper needed) and spill into the cells below from a single formula.

In adtech work, generators are how you build the *skeleton* of a workbook before any campaign data flows in. The month-axis row at the top of a 12-month projection model. The A/B bucket assigned to each campaign in an experiment tracker. The numbered rank column on a leaderboard tab. Once you have these three under your fingers, you stop hand-typing those structures and you stop dragging the fill handle.

## SEQUENCE: a counted list

`SEQUENCE` builds a column, row, or rectangle of consecutive numbers. The simplest call is `=SEQUENCE(10)`: that spills 1 through 10 down a column from one cell. Reach for it whenever you need a numbered axis (month numbers, day offsets, rank positions) without typing the values by hand.

The full signature gives you a start value and a step too, so the same function builds `0, 30, 60, ...` or `100, 95, 90, ...` just as easily.

### Combined example: SEQUENCE + EOMONTH for a month axis

This is where SEQUENCE earns its place in real workbooks. Pair it with `EOMONTH` and you can build the date row at the top of a 12-month projection table from one formula, with no hand-typed dates that drift the moment someone copies the model into next year.

```
=ARRAYFORMULA(EOMONTH(DATE(2026,1,1), SEQUENCE(12)-1))
```

`SEQUENCE(12)` is 1..12. Subtract 1 to get 0..11. `EOMONTH(start, 0)` returns the last day of the start month; `EOMONTH(start, 1)` is the last day of the next month. The combination spills twelve month-end dates: `2026-01-31, 2026-02-28, 2026-03-31, 2026-04-30, ...`. The `ARRAYFORMULA` wrapper is needed because `EOMONTH` is not array-aware on its own, even though `SEQUENCE` is.

<TryIt

  task={<>Build a 12-row column of month-end dates for 2026 in column H, starting at <code>H1</code>. Combine <code>SEQUENCE(12)</code> with <code>EOMONTH</code> and <code>DATE(2026,1,1)</code>. Wrap with <code>ARRAYFORMULA</code> so EOMONTH broadcasts.</>}
  hint={<>EOMONTH adds a number of months to a start date and returns the last day of the resulting month. <code>SEQUENCE(12)-1</code> gives 0..11. Pass that as the months argument.</>}
  answer={<>This is the generator pattern at its best: one formula produces twelve calibrated rows. EOMONTH needs <code>ARRAYFORMULA</code> because, unlike <code>SEQUENCE</code>, it is not array-aware. ## RANDARRAY: random numbers

`RANDARRAY` returns a spilled grid of random decimals between 0 and 1. It is *volatile*: every edit anywhere in the workbook recalculates it, so the values shift on every keystroke. Reach for it when you want randomized order for A/B bucket assignment, sampling, or simulations, and plan from the start how you will freeze the result once you are happy with it.

Important: Sheets' `RANDARRAY` is intentionally simpler than Excel's. The Sheets signature is `RANDARRAY([rows], [columns])` only: no min, max, or integer arguments. To get an integer in a custom range, wrap the decimal output with arithmetic and `INT`.

<FunctionRef name="RANDARRAY" />

### Worked example 1: toy data

The integer-range recipe. Five random integers between 1 and 100, generated from one formula in `A2`. Press the play button to advance the spill cells.

<RefDemo
  data={[["Roll"]]}
  formula="=ARRAYFORMULA(INT(RANDARRAY(5,1)*100)+1)"
  formulaCell="A2"
  fillTo="A6"
  expectedFills={["=ARRAYFORMULA(INT(RANDARRAY(5,1)*100)+1)", "", "", "", ""]}
  caption="RANDARRAY(5,1) gives 5 decimals in [0, 1). Multiply by 100, take INT, add 1: random integers in 1..100."
/>

The general recipe is `ARRAYFORMULA(INT(RANDARRAY(rows, 1) * (max - min + 1)) + min)`. Memorize this; you will reach for it more often than you would guess.

### Worked example 2: real adtech data

The classic adtech use of RANDARRAY: bucket every campaign into A or B for a 50/50 test. One formula, one column, ready to copy-paste-values once the assignment is locked.

## MAKEARRAY: per-cell custom function

`MAKEARRAY(rows, columns, lambda)` builds a grid by calling a `LAMBDA` once for each cell. The lambda receives the cell's row and column indices (1-indexed) and returns one value per call.

It is the most programmer-flavored of the three generators and shows up most often in workbooks that lean on the `LAMBDA` helper family (`MAP`, `REDUCE`, `BYROW`, `BYCOL`, `MAKEARRAY`: covered in depth in lessons 1.18 and 1.19). For now, know that it exists, recognize its shape, and reach for it when you need a grid that depends on position rather than on existing data.

### When you would reach for it in real workbooks

The honest answer: rarely on its own. `MAKEARRAY` shines when you need a grid where every cell is a function of its position: coordinate tables for heatmaps, scenario matrices where each cell is the outcome for a `(cost tier × ROI tier)` pair, synthetic test data for a new dashboard before the real `IMPORTRANGE` lands. In day-to-day adtech work you will reach for `MAP` and `REDUCE` more often, because those operate on data you already have. Treat `MAKEARRAY` as the "generate from scratch" cousin in the same family; lessons 1.18 and 1.19 cover the family in depth.

## Used in the wild

- **Month-axis rows.** `ARRAYFORMULA(EOMONTH(DATE(yr,1,1), SEQUENCE(12)-1))` is the canonical pattern for the date row at the top of every monthly projection sheet.
- **A/B bucket columns.** `RANDARRAY` once, paste-as-values, done. Every adtech team has a frozen bucket column in their experiment tracker; the freeze step is non-optional.
- **Position numbering.** `SEQUENCE(COUNTA(B2:B))` numbers the campaigns 1..N so a leaderboard tab can rank without a stable ID column in the source.
- **Spot-check sampling.** Add a `RAND()` helper column, then `SORTN(campaign_range, 100, 0, helper, TRUE)` gives you 100 random rows for quality review. Paste-as-values to lock the sample.
- **Scenario matrices.** `MAKEARRAY` builds a `(cost tier × ROI tier)` grid where each cell is computed from the two indices: sensitivity analysis on pricing decks without a hand-typed grid.

## Pro pitfalls

- **`RANDARRAY` and `RAND` are volatile.** Every edit anywhere in the workbook recalculates them. For a stable assignment, paste-as-values immediately. If a shared dashboard depends on the column, freeze it *before* sharing the file.
- **`SEQUENCE` and `RANDARRAY` spill, and anything in the way breaks the formula.** Type `=SEQUENCE(12)` in `H1` when `H5` already has a value and the whole formula errors with `#REF!` and a spill-conflict message. Clear the spill range first, or move the formula.
- **`SEQUENCE(1000000)` will lock up the sheet.** It really does generate a million-row column and chews through memory. Cap the count to what you actually need; pair with `COUNTA` rather than `ROWS` on open-ended ranges.
- **Sheets' `RANDARRAY` does not take min, max, or integer arguments.** Excel's does. Copy-pasted recipes from Excel sources return `#N/A`. The Sheets idiom is `INT(RANDARRAY(rows, 1) * (max - min + 1)) + min`.
- **Generators do not survive `IMPORTRANGE` cleanly.** If the source workbook has a live `RANDARRAY` column and a destination imports it, every refresh shifts the values in the destination too. Paste-as-values in the source before the import lands. Same caution applies to `NOW`/`TODAY` columns crossing workbook boundaries.
- **`MAKEARRAY` lambdas must return a scalar per call.** If the lambda body returns a range, you get `#VALUE!`. Use `BYROW` or `REDUCE` (lesson 1.19) when you need lambdas that emit arrays.

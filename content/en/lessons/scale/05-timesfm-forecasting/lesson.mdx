# Forecasting with TimesFM in Connected Sheets

For most of the history of Sheets, forecasting was a wall: either you used `FORECAST.ETS` on a column of numbers and prayed the seasonality matched what your campaigns actually did, or you exported to Python. As of early 2026, Connected Sheets has a third option that quietly changes the math: a single point-and-click "Forecast" action that calls **TimesFM** through BigQuery ML and lands the predictions back on a new tab. No SQL. No model training. No Python.

This lesson is about using that flow on the kind of daily-spend series the team actually keeps, and reading the result like the practitioner you are rather than the believer the demo wants you to be.

## What TimesFM actually is

TimesFM (Time Series Foundation Model) is a Google-trained foundation model for time-series data, released as a paper in October 2024 and productized inside BigQuery ML during 2025. It does for time series what Gemini does for text: one model, many series, no per-series training. You feed it a column of timestamps and a column of numeric values, you tell it how far forward to predict (the horizon), and it returns a table of predictions plus confidence intervals.

In Connected Sheets, Google wrapped this into a UI:

1. Open the Connected Sheets table tied to your BigQuery table (or extract).
2. Click **Preview** at the top of the table to enter the preview view.
3. Click **Advanced Analytics → Create a Forecast**.
4. Pick the timestamp column, the value column, the horizon (1 to 90 days), and the confidence level.
5. Click **Create**.
6. A new tab appears with the predicted values, a lower confidence bound, and an upper confidence bound.

The BigQuery side runs an `AI.FORECAST` call against the hosted TimesFM endpoint. Billing is per row scanned plus the BigQuery ML inference cost; on the kind of daily-spend series this team runs (under 1000 rows) a single forecast costs cents. The Google docs page covering the launch is the source of truth on pricing and limits.

## The no-SQL workflow, with a real series

The team's daily-spend log on the aggregator workbook has six columns and 30 days of rows: Date, Spend, Revenue, Clicks, Impressions, Conversions. To produce a 7-day spend forecast:

- Make sure the log is a Connected Sheets table (it is, in the team's workbook, because the source is a BigQuery dataset).
- Forecast → Spend over Date → horizon 7 → confidence 0.95.
- A new tab `Forecast` lands with three columns: forecast_timestamp, forecast_value, prediction_interval_lower_bound, prediction_interval_upper_bound.

That tab is just a sheet. You can `=Forecast!B2` from anywhere, pipe it into a daily-budget alert, chart it next to the actuals, or hand it to a `QUERY` for a weekly summary. The forecast is materialized, which means it doesn't recompute every time the workbook opens. You refresh it by clicking Forecast again.

## Horizon, confidence intervals, and what "the forecast" actually is

The most common mistake when learning a forecasting tool is treating the predicted value as a deterministic estimate. The point estimate is a number. The forecast is the *interval*. TimesFM returns a lower bound and an upper bound at your chosen confidence level. On a daily-spend series with the kind of volatility this team sees, the 95% interval for tomorrow can easily be plus or minus 30% of the point estimate. By day 14 it's plus or minus 80%. By day 30 it's a coin flip.

Two rules for reading the output:

1. **Quote the interval, not the point.** "TimesFM forecasts tomorrow's spend at \$3,440, with a 95% interval of \$2,900 to \$3,980" is honest. "TimesFM says \$3,440" is the kind of confidence that ends meetings badly.
2. **Stop reading past day 14.** The model is calibrated for short horizons. Past day 14 the confidence interval grows faster than the point estimate moves; you are reading noise.

## When TimesFM beats classical ARIMA

Classical forecasting (ARIMA, ETS, Holt-Winters) needs you to specify seasonality, trend, and differencing parameters. On a daily-spend series with weekly seasonality plus an irregular launch-day spike (the team's actual pattern, mid-month launches that pull in tens of percent of spend overnight), classical models either miss the spike or over-fit it. TimesFM was trained on hundreds of billions of time-series points and picks up weekly seasonality, holiday effects, and irregular spikes without tuning. The 2026-04-15 launch-day spike in this lesson's seed series is exactly the kind of structure TimesFM handles cleanly and ARIMA-without-tuning misses.

Where TimesFM loses:

- **Very short series.** Under 14 days of history is too little context. TimesFM falls back to something close to a smoothed mean.
- **Strong known seasonal anchors.** If you already know your series is dominated by a clean monthly billing cycle, a tuned ARIMA with that seasonality declared can match TimesFM with much smaller compute.
- **Series with sharp regime changes.** If you launched a new vertical mid-series and the spend pattern fundamentally changed, neither approach works. Refit on the new regime.

## Used in the wild

- **Tomorrow's-budget alert.** The team's daily-budget alert reads `Forecast!B2` (tomorrow's predicted spend) plus the upper bound from `Forecast!D2`. If the upper bound exceeds the day's budget by 20%, Apps Script emails the buyer at 9pm. The forecast feeds a decision; the buyer doesn't read the model.
- **Launch-day detection.** The 2026-04-15 spike in the seed series is what a new winning campaign looks like: spend jumps 22% overnight, holds. TimesFM rolls the spike into the trend by day 3; a naive 7-day moving average needs a week to catch up. Saving four days of under-budgeted serving on a winning vertical pays for the model many times over.
- **Weekly close.** Every Sunday, the dashboard generates a fresh 7-day forecast and compares the previous week's predictions against the actuals. Persistent over-prediction on a vertical is a signal that something changed at the platform; persistent under-prediction is a signal that the vertical is scaling and budgets need a bump.

## Pro pitfalls

- **Treating the point estimate as the answer.** It isn't. The deliverable is the interval. Decisions need to survive the lower bound, not just match the midpoint.
- **Extrapolating past 14 days.** The model degrades fast on long horizons. A 30-day forecast on a daily-spend series is decoration, not data.
- **Forecasts that look right but are zero-anchored.** TimesFM assumes the historical pattern continues. If a campaign ended mid-week, the model doesn't know; the prediction follows the recent rate as if the campaign were still serving. Always cross-check the predicted days against the campaigns you actually have live.
- **Re-forecasting on every workbook open.** TimesFM is a BigQuery ML inference. Each refresh costs money. The output tab is materialized for a reason; refresh on a schedule, not on every read.
- **Forgetting to label the model.** Three months from now somebody opens the workbook and finds the Forecast tab. Was this TimesFM? Was it ETS? Was it the intern's Python script? The cell that names the model is the cheapest documentation in the building.

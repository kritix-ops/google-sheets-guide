# Why your sheet is slow

A workbook that lagged for half a second yesterday lags for eight today. Nobody touched the formulas. The data grew by a thousand rows and a sleeping problem woke up. This lesson is the formula-level companion to lesson 2.18's workbook-level hygiene rules; lesson 2.18 was about how the whole workbook ages, this one is about which individual formulas cost the most. Three patterns account for almost every slow Sheets workbook:

1. **Volatile functions** that re-evaluate on every edit and on the minute timer.
2. **Full-column references** that walk a million empty cells on every recalc.
3. **Recalc cascades** where one cell's change triggers a chain of downstream formulas.

You need to be able to recognize each one in formula text. Once you can spot them, the fix is usually a five-second edit per formula. Pros do this audit by eye in the formula bar; you'll get there with practice.

## Volatile functions: the silent recalc tax

Six functions in Sheets are volatile: `TODAY`, `NOW`, `RAND`, `RANDBETWEEN`, `OFFSET`, `INDIRECT`. "Volatile" means Sheets recomputes them every time *any* cell anywhere in the workbook changes, plus on a one-minute timer for the time-based ones. A workbook with one `TODAY()` is fine. A workbook with `TODAY()` inside an `ARRAYFORMULA` over 50,000 rows is 50,000 volatile cells, each pulling its dependents into the recalc on every keystroke.

The two volatile lookups in particular are the ones that turn an aggregator workbook from snappy to sluggish without warning: `INDIRECT` is how teams glue dynamic tab names onto formulas, and `OFFSET` is how the older guides taught "find the last row." Both feel innocent in the formula bar.

The dangerous shapes to spot in a slow workbook:

- **`INDIRECT("Sheet1!A1")`** is volatile AND breaks the moment Sheet1 is renamed. Use a direct reference when you can.
- **`OFFSET(A1, 0, 0, COUNTA(A:A), 1)`** is the "find the last row" trick. It is volatile, and the `COUNTA(A:A)` inside is also a full-column ref. Two slow things in one formula.
- **`TODAY()` inside `ARRAYFORMULA`** is the worst one. A single `TODAY` is cheap; `ARRAYFORMULA(TODAY()-A2:A1000)` is a thousand volatile cells.

The cure is usually the same: replace the volatile call with a static value (a hardcoded date string), a cell reference to a date the user updates manually, or a non-volatile alternative (use `INDEX(A:A, n)` instead of `OFFSET`).

## Full-column references

`SUM(F:F)` looks future-proof. It also forces Sheets to read every cell in column F before computing the sum. Sheets caps a sheet at one million rows by default, so that is potentially a million cells of work to add up sixty numbers.

The fix is to bound the range explicitly: `SUM(F2:F61)`, or `SUM(F2:F5000)` if you want headroom for the next two years of growth. The common objections:

- **"What if new rows arrive?"** Bound to `F2:F5000` or use a named range that you grow when needed. The extra empty cells in `F2:F5000` are cheap because Sheets sees them as a contiguous block, not as individual addresses.
- **"`ARRAYFORMULA` lets me reference `F:F` and it still works."** Sometimes. Other times the spill exceeds your patience and the workbook freezes. Bound it.
- **"`COUNTA(B:B)` is convenient as a row count."** Yes. It is also slow. `COUNTA(B2:B5000)` does the same job at 0.5% of the work.

`File > Settings > Spreadsheet stats` shows the formula count and recalc time. If the workbook reports more than a couple of seconds, full-column references are almost always part of it.

## Recalc cascades

A single edit triggers recomputation of every cell that references it, recursively. If `J1 = SUM(F2:F61)` and `K1 = J1 / J2` and `L1 = K1 * 100` and twenty other cells reference `L1`, editing one number in F cascades through twenty-three cells. With a hundred dependent cells and a couple of volatile functions in the mix, each keystroke is hundreds of milliseconds of work.

You do not usually need to redesign the dependency graph. You need to make sure:

- **Aggregations happen once.** `J1 = SUM(F2:F61)` is one SUM. Twenty cells that each compute `SUM(F2:F61)/something` is twenty SUMs. Reuse the summary cell.
- **Conditional formats are bounded.** A conditional format that targets `A:Z` is a rule that fires across a million cells. Bound to `A1:Z1000`.
- **Data validation rules don't sit on empty cells you'll never use.** Each one runs on every edit.

## Used in the wild

The team's main attribution workbook lagged eight seconds per edit by mid-2025. Two engineers spent half a day on it. The fix:

- 41 cells with `SUM(F:F)`, `SUM(G:G)`, or `COUNTA(B:B)` patterns. Bounded all to `F2:F5000`.
- 6 cells with `INDIRECT` constructing tab names dynamically. Two were genuinely dynamic (kept as is, accepted the cost). Four were not (replaced with direct references).
- 1 conditional format rule covering `A:Z` on a sheet with 14,000 rows. Bounded to `A1:Z14000`.
- 1 `ARRAYFORMULA(TODAY() - A2:A)` that was lighting up the recalc on every minute tick. Replaced with a static `Report date` cell at the top of the sheet.

Recalc dropped from 8s to 0.6s. The workbook did not get smaller; the work per edit did.

## Pro pitfalls

- **`INDIRECT("Sheet1!A1")` is the silent assassin.** Volatile, breaks on rename, and looks innocent in a formula bar. If you find one in a workbook that lags, it is almost certainly part of the cause. Lesson 4.7 covers the cycle case where `INDIRECT` hides a circular reference.
- **A volatile function inside `ARRAYFORMULA` is N volatile cells.** `ARRAYFORMULA(TODAY()-A2:A1000)` is 999 volatile cells.
- **"`OFFSET` to find the last row" is the old guide answer.** It is also volatile and slow. Use `INDEX(A:A, COUNTA(A:A))` or, better, name a range and grow it manually.
- **`File > Settings > Spreadsheet stats` is your friend.** It tells you the cell count, formula count, IMPORTRANGE count, and how many of those are volatile. Look at it whenever a workbook feels sluggish.
- **A formula bar full of nested IFs is fine.** The slow thing is not formula complexity; it is range size and volatility. Do not refactor a clean `IFS` chain; refactor the `SUM(F:F)` two rows down.

Lesson 4.3 takes the bounded-range discipline one layer up: every `IMPORTRANGE` the team owns lives or dies by the same rule, with the added twist that the source workbook can change shape under you between refreshes.

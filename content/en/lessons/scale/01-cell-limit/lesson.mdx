# The 10M cell limit and what hits it

Google Sheets caps a workbook at 10 million cells OR 18,278 columns (column ZZZ), whichever you hit first. Not 10 million *filled* cells. Ten million cells, period. The empty ones count. A tab allocated as `A1:ZZ50000` burns 1.7M cells out of your budget before anyone types a digit. This is the limit that quietly kills aggregator workbooks: the team adds a platform, an IMPORTRANGE drags more rows than expected, somebody pastes a 200,000-row export onto an existing tab, and one morning Sheets refuses to insert another row.

(As of April 2026, an opt-in beta doubles the cap to 20 million cells for participating domains, and across-the-board performance gains have arrived for all users: ~30% faster sheet open at 1M+ cells, ~60% faster filtering, ~60% faster conditional formatting. The column ceiling and the per-tab default grid have not changed. Most teams reading this lesson are still on the 10M cap until an admin opts the domain in, so treat 10M as the planning number and 20M as headroom if and when your domain gets enrolled.)

The rule to internalize: **what counts is not the data, it is the grid.** Sheets bills you for cells you allocated, not cells you used. Until you understand that, every workbook the team owns is one paste away from the wall. The bounded-range discipline from lesson 2.1 (and the `IMPORTRANGE` patterns from lesson 1.27) are the upstream defenses that keep grids from drifting in the first place.

## How Sheets allocates cells

When you open a new tab, Sheets gives it a default grid (currently 26 columns and 1000 rows = 26,000 cells). When you paste data wider or taller than that grid, Sheets extends the grid to fit, subject to two hard ceilings: 10 million cells across the workbook (20 million on the April 2026 opt-in beta) AND 18,278 columns (column ZZZ). You hit whichever ceiling comes first. A workbook with one huge wide tab can hit the column ceiling long before the cell ceiling; a workbook with many medium tabs hits the cell ceiling first. The grid never shrinks on its own. Delete the rows and the grid stays. Hide the columns and the grid stays. The only way to give cells back is to right-click the row or column header and choose **Delete row/column**, which actually removes the allocation rather than just clearing the content.

A few patterns drift the budget without anyone noticing:

- **The paste that quietly extends.** Someone pastes a 50,000-row export onto `Raw - Taboola`. Sheets extends the tab from 1000 rows to 50,000 rows. Two weeks later they delete the data but the grid still has 50,000 rows.
- **The 40-column tab that uses 7.** Wide pivots and wide raw imports leave columns AA through AN allocated and empty. Each one is 1000+ cells (or however tall the tab is) sitting on your budget.
- **`ARRAYFORMULA` that's wider than expected.** A spilling `ARRAYFORMULA` extends the grid down to its furthest output cell. If the formula spills to row 100,000 because someone wrote `IFERROR(..., "")` over a column, the grid carries that to 100,000.
- **`IMPORTRANGE` without a bounded range.** `IMPORTRANGE(url, "Sheet1!A:Z")` pulls the full source sheet. If the source has 30,000 rows, your tab now has 30,000 rows too.

## Finding the waste

The cheap audit is two numbers per tab:

```
=COUNTA(A2:lastRow)              filled cells in your useful payload
=COLUMNS(A:Z) * ROWS(A1:A1000)   allocated grid for this tab
```

Their difference is wasted cells. Their ratio is your efficiency. A tab where useful cells are 1% of allocated is a tab costing you 99% of its budget for nothing.

Run this across every tab. Sheets exposes the totals under **File > Settings > Spreadsheet stats** (cell count, formula count, IMPORTRANGE count). Anything over 5M cells is a workbook in the danger zone; anything over 8M needs an archive cycle this week.

## What happens when you hit the limit

The error reads "This action would increase the number of cells in the workbook above the limit of 10000000 cells." Common ways to trip it:

- Inserting rows. Even one row at a time.
- Pasting a block that would extend the grid.
- Adding a tab. A fresh tab with the default 26x1000 grid alone is 26,000 cells.
- An `IMPORTRANGE` that pulls more rows than fit. The cell shows `#REF!` with "Result too large" in the tooltip.

There is no soft warning. The workbook simply stops accepting growth.

## Used in the wild

The team's aggregator workbook started at five platforms and seven tabs. Each tab was `A1:Z1000`. Total allocation: 7 * 26,000 = 182,000 cells. Two years later it had 14 platforms, 21 tabs, several with 50,000 rows from old IMPORTRANGE pulls that never got bounded, and one tab someone had extended to column ZZ for a one-off pivot in 2024. Total allocation: 4.8M cells. The fix was not a rewrite. It was an afternoon: delete the rows below the live data on each Raw tab, delete the columns past the schema, archive the 2024 months to a separate workbook, replace `IMPORTRANGE(url, "A:Z")` with `IMPORTRANGE(url, "A1:G5000")`. The workbook dropped to 900K cells and recalc time dropped from eleven seconds to under two.

## Pro pitfalls

- **Pasting into a column-locked range silently extends the sheet.** A protected range covers `A:G`, you paste a 12,000-row block in, and Sheets extends the tab down to row 12,000. The paste does not violate the protection (it is pasting into the allowed columns) but the grid grew.
- **`IMPORTRANGE` without a bounded second argument pulls the full source sheet.** If the source has a hidden 200,000-row archive, your aggregator just inherited it. Always bound: `IMPORTRANGE(url, "Sheet1!A1:G5000")`. Lesson 4.3 makes this the team default.
- **Charts that auto-extend their data range.** A chart pointed at `A:F` re-reads the entire column on every refresh. Combined with an IMPORTRANGE source, this is a budget and recalc time hit.
- **Hidden columns and hidden rows are NOT deleted.** They still consume cells. The audit catches them; the eye does not.
- **A new tab is 26,000 cells.** Twenty utility tabs that no one uses anymore is 520,000 cells of pure waste.

Lesson 4.2 picks up where this one leaves off: once the grid is sized right, the next reason a workbook feels slow is volatile formulas and full-column references. Same audit mindset, different culprits.
